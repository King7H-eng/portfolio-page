## About this project

A five-person group project at Durham University to build a 3D-printed tail-sitter eVTOL drone, designed to take off vertically and cruise as a fixed-wing aircraft on autonomous wildlife-monitoring and anti-poaching patrols. The project covered the airframe build and the integration of the flight controller, power and navigation hardware, followed by flight testing and crash analysis. Autonomous patrol missions were scripted in Lua and tested in a Mission Planner simulation, and the project closed with a critical review of the design and recommendations for future development. Ultimately, the drone failed to take off and land successfully in one piece.

**My role:** Team leader and drone pilot. Through the project I became an A2 CofC qualified drone pilot.

## Executive summary

This Report covers the construction and analysis of a fixed-wing eVTOL platform designed by Eclipson. The aim of the project was to build a long-range and efficient low-noise aircraft, capable of conducting patrols of a reserve without the need for a runway.

The design features a tail-sitter VTOL configuration for vertical take-off and fixed-wing cruise. The drone's airframe was fabricated using 3D printers, and all other parts and equipment were supplied by Durham University. Both Mission Planner and LUA code were used to integrate the software into the platform, providing autonomous capabilities. Despite correctly adhering to the set-up guide, published by Eclipson, multiple structural limitations stopped us from achieving the goal of transitioning to fixed-wing cruise flight.

Specifically, weaknesses between the wings and fuselage that resulted in difficulty to land without the drone suffering significant damage. Large time delays came as a consequence of the entire wing being re-printed. This made it hard to troubleshoot calibration and sensitivity issues in the given time frame.

During the test flight we encountered minor errors surrounding hardware, GPS calibration and electromagnetic interference. Alone, these were reparable issues, but due to a lack of repairability, the iterative troubleshooting process took longer than expected.

Recommendations were made for improvements to both the design, and the build process. These included suggestions to improve the strength, feasibility and performance of the drone.

The total cost of the drone's components was £512.28.

## System architecture

The drone's electronics were built around a power distribution board and a Pixhawk 6C Mini flight controller running ArduPlane. The autonomous missions were scripted in Lua and tested only in simulation.

## What went wrong, and why

The drone never achieved a stable flight. Each failure was investigated from flight footage, logs and a critical review of the design, and the report sets out what was changed and what is recommended for a future iteration.
