## Executive summary

Air cooling is the cheapest way to keep an electric-vehicle battery pack cool, but it has a flaw. The air warms up as it passes each cell, so cells at the far end run hotter than those at the front. This research project tested whether a thin coating on each cell could fix that without using any extra energy.

The coating (a porous material called MOF-801) holds water from the air. When a cell heats up, the coating releases that water, which carries heat away, much like sweat cooling skin. When the cell cools, the coating soaks the water back up, ready for the next cycle.

I built a MATLAB simulation of a coated battery cell, checked it against lab measurements (average error under 1 °C), and scaled it up to a row of 15 air-cooled cells.

**Headline results** (fast-charging case):
- Peak temperature rise cut by **30.5%** (7.4 °C)
- Temperature spread between cells cut by **43.2%** (4.1 °C)
- Trade-off: cells take **68.7%** longer to cool back to room temperature

**Key insight:** the cells help each other through the shared airflow. Coatings on the hottest cells, at the back of the row, release more water and remove up to **1.7×** more heat than those at the front, which evens out the temperatures. The study also identified when the coating stops helping: above about 1 m/s airflow, and in very dry air.

## Context

Battery cells need to stay between 25 and 40 °C, with less than 5 °C between cells. Excess heat causes degradation, a shorter lifespan and, in extreme cases, thermal runaway. Redesigning the airflow channels helps, but only by making the pack bigger or adding inlets, which means more airflow and more energy. Coatings like this had shown early promise, but their behaviour at pack scale had not been characterised.

## Methodology

**1. Electrical model of the cell**
- Represented the battery as a simple equivalent circuit (a resistor plus a resistor–capacitor pair) to predict voltage and heat output as it charges
- Tracked state of charge by integrating current over time
- Made the resistances temperature-dependent with exponential (Arrhenius) relationships
- Computed heat generated from electrical losses plus the cell's internal chemical reactions

**2. Heat flow inside the cell**
- Solved the transient heat-conduction PDE in cylindrical coordinates, radially from the cell's core out through its steel casing and the coating
- Discretised with the **finite-volume method** across three layers: heat-generating core, casing, coating
- **Implicit time integration** (θ = 1.0) with a 0.05 s time step

**3. How the coating absorbs and releases water**
- **How much water it can hold:** an established empirical equation from the literature for how much water the coating holds at a given temperature and humidity. This equation also gives the heat absorbed or released per gram of water.
- **How fast it responds:** a first-order rate equation, where uptake moves towards its equilibrium value at a speed that rises exponentially with temperature
- **Coupling:** water released by the coating becomes a heat sink in the coating layer of the heat-conduction model

**4. Pack and airflow model**
- Split the airstream into one control volume per cell, each tracking air temperature and humidity
- Air temperature rise past each cell computed with the **NTU (number of transfer units) method**
- A mass balance carried the water each coating releases or absorbs downstream, so every cell affects the cells behind it
- Heat-transfer coefficients from standard empirical correlations for forced and natural convection

**5. Boundary conditions**
- Neumann (zero heat flux) at the cell centre, by symmetry
- Robin at the outer surface: heat conducted out equals heat convected away
- Dirichlet at the air inlet: room temperature and humidity

**6. Validation**
- Compared predictions with lab surface-temperature data for a commercial cell, coated and uncoated, at three charging and three discharging rates
- Error metrics: RMSE, weighted RMSE, mean absolute error, maximum error and peak error

**7. Analysis**
- Parameter sweeps of airflow speed
- **One-at-a-time sensitivity analysis** across room temperature, humidity and airflow speed

## Results

- **Single cell:** the coating cut peak temperature by up to 9.6 °C when overcharging, and by about 4 °C during normal fast charging
- **Pack:** 30.5% lower peak temperature rise and 43.2% smaller spread between cells, compared with an uncoated, air-cooled pack
- **Why it works:** air warms as it moves along the row, so coatings on the back cells release more water. The cell at the back removed 1.7× as much heat as the cell at the front.
- **Limits:**
  - Above 1 m/s airflow, the coating's insulating effect outweighs its cooling
  - At very slow airflow (0.05 m/s), the air becomes saturated with water and can't take any more
  - Below ~35% humidity, the coating holds too little water to start with
- **Headroom:** the coating used only about 11% of its theoretical cooling capacity, so it could handle much larger heat loads
