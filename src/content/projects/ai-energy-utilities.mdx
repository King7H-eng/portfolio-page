## Market commentary

With markets debating whether the AI hyper-growth cycle will persist or collapse like the dot-com bubble, one sector sits uncomfortably in the middle: US energy utilities. The American grid, already ageing, congested and slow to expand, is now being asked to support the fastest load growth the country has seen in nearly fifty years. Data-centre demand is rising far faster than utilities can build for, leaving them caught between overbuilding or capping the AI industry's growth. The core problem is simple: no one can reliably forecast how much electricity these new AI-heavy data centres will actually require as seen in figure 1.

Much of today's projected demand is based on speculative or duplicative interconnection requests rather than firm commitments. Developers routinely submit multiple, nearly identical grid-connection applications for a single data-centre concept in different locations, shopping for better timelines, pricing or permitting environments. Every utility sees the request in its own queue, inflating both apparent regional demand and capacity needs.

Some developers also submit what regulators call "phantom" data centres: projects without land rights, planning permission or financing, filed simply to hold a place in the queue while alternatives are evaluated. In effect, the queue is no longer a pipeline of real projects; it is a menu of options.

When Oxford Economics audited a national dataset, they found that after removing duplicates and low-probability projects, only one in five data-centre requests reflected a realistic build-out. In certain markets, industry insiders estimate that some operators file dozens of parallel interconnection requests for a single prospective site. Across parts of the US, only 20-30% of projects in interconnection queues ever get built. That means current load projections in some regions may be 3 to 5 times higher than what will materialise based on the audit.

AEP Ohio illustrates the problem. The utility has confirmed around 24 GW of load commitments to fulfil by the end of the decade, while customers are actively seeking another 190 GW of potential demand, roughly five times its existing system. On closer inspection, much of this interest is unlikely to proceed, yet AEP must still plan around it. In response, the company has introduced a tariff that requires large customers to pay for at least 85% of the energy they subscribe to, even if they do not ultimately use it. Variations of this dynamic are now emerging across multiple US utilities.

The system straining under this pressure was designed in the mid-twentieth century for slow, predictable growth, not for AI clusters drawing hundreds of megawatts per campus. By contrast, constructing the infrastructure to supply them is painfully slow. New high-voltage transmission lines typically require seven to ten years from planning to energisation, while some upgrades face permitting timelines exceeding a decade. Hardware bottlenecks are worsening delays, procurement of large power transformers now carries three- to four-year lead times, and specialised HVDC cables can take five years or more to source. Federal estimate that the grid will require 2 trillion dollars in grid investment to provide this predicted AI demand.

Being conservative with infrastructure capex risks the United States falling behind in the AI arms race. Overbuilding, however, exposes utilities and consumers to stranded assets, higher tariffs and political backlash if demand deflates. The uncomfortable reality is that even in the bear case for AI energy demand, the US grid is already critically undersized. The challenge is no longer whether to build, but how to build fast enough, in the right places, before the bottleneck tightens.

For investors, monitoring policy developments and how utilities build out capacity will be critical. Companies with disciplined queue management are better placed to gauge real demand, deliver steadier returns and avoid stranded assets.

Infrastructure suppliers with multi-year backlogs for high-demand equipment such as transformers also stand to benefit. They typically require significant payment upfront and operate in a constrained supply chain, partially insulating them from whether the AI boom continues at its current pace or moderates.

The utilities to be most nuanced on are those taking a "build and pray" approach: ramping capex aggressively based on inflated queues without adequate contractual protection. These names will perform well if AI demand fully materialises, but if growth fails to continue exponentially they risk stranded assets and elevated capex tied to multi-year infrastructure that will not generate the revenues originally expected.

In this cycle, the winners are likely to be the utilities and equipment providers that capture AI-driven growth while pricing in the reality that much of today's data-centre demand is still only on paper.
