## Executive summary

AI data centres are driving the fastest US electricity demand growth in nearly fifty years. But no one can reliably forecast how much power they will actually need. Interconnection queues are inflated by duplicate applications and "phantom" projects, and an Oxford Economics audit found only about 1 in 5 data-centre requests reflected a realistic build-out. Utilities are caught between overbuilding and capping the AI industry's growth, on a grid where new transmission takes 7–10 years.

**Conclusion of the note:** even in the bear case for AI energy demand, the US grid is already critically undersized. The challenge is no longer whether to build, but how to build fast enough, in the right places.

**Investment view:**
- **Better placed:** utilities with disciplined queue management, which can gauge real demand, deliver steadier returns and avoid stranded assets
- **Better placed:** infrastructure suppliers with multi-year backlogs for high-demand equipment such as transformers. They typically take significant payment upfront, which partly insulates them from how the AI boom plays out.
- **Be nuanced on:** "build and pray" utilities that ramp capex on inflated queues without adequate contractual protection

## Context

Markets are debating whether the AI hyper-growth cycle will persist or collapse like the dot-com bubble. US energy utilities sit uncomfortably in the middle.

## Methodology

**1. Identify the core uncertainty.** No one can reliably forecast how much electricity AI data centres will need.

**2. Explain why demand projections are inflated.**
- Developers submit multiple near-identical grid applications for a single data-centre concept in different locations
- "Phantom" data centres are filed without land rights, planning permission or financing
- Only 20–30% of projects in interconnection queues ever get built

**3. Quantify the gap with evidence.**
- Oxford Economics audit: only ~1 in 5 data-centre requests realistic, so load projections in some regions may be 3–5× too high
- Case study, AEP Ohio: ~24 GW of confirmed commitments vs ~190 GW of potential demand sought, roughly five times its existing system. The utility responded with a tariff requiring large customers to pay for at least 85% of the energy they subscribe to.

**4. Set out the supply-side constraints.**
- High-voltage transmission: 7–10 years from planning to energisation
- Large power transformers: 3–4 year lead times
- Specialised HVDC cables: 5+ years
- Federal estimate: $2tn of grid investment needed

**5. Weigh the risks either way.** Underbuilding risks the US falling behind in the AI race. Overbuilding risks stranded assets, higher tariffs and political backlash. Even in the bear case, the grid is undersized.

**6. Translate into investment positioning** (see Executive summary above).

**7. Data visualisation.** A chart of global data-centre electricity consumption forecasts, built in Python with Matplotlib.
